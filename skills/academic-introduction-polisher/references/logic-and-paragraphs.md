# Introduction Logic and Paragraph Architecture

## Default research-article architecture

Use four or five paragraphs when the material supports it. Paragraph count is not a formal requirement; all logical functions are.

### Paragraph 1 — Phenomenon and significance

Define the specific phenomenon, scientific setting, and why it matters. Prefer a quantitative observation, physical process, or bounded problem. Exclude generic discipline-level statements unless the audience genuinely needs them.

### Paragraph 2 — Established knowledge and mechanism framework

State what is already known and organize literature by mechanisms, competing explanations, scales, or evidence types. A paragraph should synthesize judgments rather than narrate papers one by one.

### Paragraph 3 — Precise gap

Build the gap from a contradiction, omitted process, unknown variable, unresolved relative contribution, scale mismatch, causal ambiguity, or observational limitation. A strong gap explains both what is unknown and why current evidence cannot resolve it.

Useful gap types:

- unknown magnitude, distribution, timing, or boundary condition;
- uncertain relationship between two processes;
- mechanisms that make similar observable predictions;
- laboratory-to-field or event-to-regional scale mismatch;
- missing coupling, geometry, heterogeneity, or time dependence;
- conflicting conclusions caused by differing assumptions;
- one-way causal framing that neglects feedback.

### Paragraph 4 — Study design as the answer to the gap

Map each dataset or method to a limitation stated in Paragraph 3. Explain why it constrains an unknown or distinguishes hypotheses. Avoid a bare tool list.

### Paragraph 5 — Objective, principal finding, or contribution

State the central question and, when journal conventions allow, preview the principal result. End at the mechanism or framework level rather than “we studied region X.” Keep the claim strength consistent with the evidence.

## Compact variants

### Nature, Science, and short Letters

Often compress to two or three functional paragraphs:

1. phenomenon + standard explanation + tension;
2. evidence limitation + unique opportunity;
3. study action + principal claim.

Compression does not justify skipping the gap.

### Perspective

Use: mainstream focus → neglected object/process → why it matters → proposed conceptual revision. Prefer `we argue` or `we propose` over result verbs when the contribution is interpretive.

### Review

Use: importance → terminology/scope → why evidence is hard to compare → inclusion or classification framework → review roadmap. State evidence heterogeneity and inclusion rules explicitly when relevant.

## Logic tests

Before finalizing, verify:

- Each paragraph has one primary rhetorical job.
- Every paragraph's final sentence prepares the next paragraph.
- The gap follows from the reviewed evidence rather than appearing abruptly.
- Every major method answers a named limitation.
- Removing a method would leave a specific question unanswered.
- The contribution is one conceptual level above the case description.
- Contrast words connect genuinely conflicting propositions.
- Causal language does not exceed the evidence.

## Common repairs

- Replace a generic opening with the paper's actual phenomenon.
- Convert an author-by-author list into grouped scientific claims.
- Replace “few studies” with the missing quantity or unresolved relationship.
- Move excessive site description out of the opening unless it creates the scientific problem.
- Remove detailed procedure from the Introduction; retain only its gap-closing role.
- Split sentences that contain background, gap, method, and result simultaneously.
